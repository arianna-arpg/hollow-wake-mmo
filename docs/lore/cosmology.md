# Cosmology: The Loom and the Nine Threads

## The Loom

Everything that exists is woven. This is not a figure of speech in Aureth; it is the plain nature of things, which Weavers can see and everyone else can feel. The whole of reality, the sky and what is under the sky and what is under that, is one working called the Loom. Nothing is outside it except the Void, which is the room it was given.

The Loom has a Warp and a Weft. The Warp is the fixed part: the long threads laid first, which do not move. People see the Warp most clearly at night, in the northern sky, where the stars sit in rows and columns too regular to be chance; sailors steer by them and call that quarter of the sky simply the Warp. The Weft is the moving part: the days, the weather, the lives of creatures, everything that crosses the Warp and is woven in. Marcher folk call the sun the Shuttle because it carries the day back and forth across the Warp, and the moon the Bobbin because it winds and unwinds.

The edge of the cloth is the Selvage. Travellers who claim to have reached it say the world simply stops, in a clean hem of light, as if finished rather than ended. No one has brought back proof. The five days at the end of each year, which belong to no month, are also called the Selvage, because they are the edge of the year.

## The Loomwright

Someone set the Loom. Aureth was made on purpose. The evidence is everywhere once a person learns to look: the Warp in the sky, rivers that lie across the land in long laid curves like thread put down by hand, hills in the Hallows that fold on one another like cloth on a table, a staircase of fused glass in the Emberfells that climbs a cliff to nothing, and the fact that there are exactly nine Threads and not eight or ten. The maker is not present. Nobody in any surviving record has met them. The world has the feeling of a house that was tidied by someone who then went out: the table set, the lamps trimmed, the door left open.

Scholars in Ilvary call the maker the Loomwright, and that is the word used across these files when no particular culture is speaking. The peoples of Aureth have their own names, and the names say what each people believes:

- Marchers say the Quiet Hand, and say grace to "the Hand that set the table and went out."
- The Mending Houses of Ilvary say the Absent, and build their liturgy around an empty chair.
- The Wakefolk say Whose Wake We Sail, and hold that the western sea is the wake the maker left in leaving.
- The Durn say the Slow, and hold that the maker never left but is in the stone, and simply moves at the speed of stone.
- The Loosed say the Dropped Shuttle, and hold that the maker let go of the Loom on purpose so that it could weave itself.

No faith in Aureth worships the Loomwright as a god who listens. The common wisdom of Weavers is a hard little saying: the Loom does not love you, but it answers.

## The Nine Threads

There are nine Threads, and every Pattern in the world is made of some mixture of them. A stone is almost all Stone with a little Ember remembered from the fire that made it. A person is all nine in a proportion that is theirs alone. The Threads are not gods, though folk-tales treat them like people, and they are not opposed pairs, though some sit more easily together than others. Each has a character, a way it is felt by people who cannot see it, places and people it gathers, and a way of going wrong when it frays.

A Weaver sees the Threads as a kind of light with a grain to it, each with its own colour and movement. The colours given below are the ones commonly reported; they are a convention for artists, not a rule of the world.

### Ember

Fire, heat, hunger, the moment of catching. Ember is the Thread of change by consumption: whatever it touches becomes something else and gives off warmth in the going. It is seen as red gold with a rising grain.

How it is felt: the hearth, the smell of smoke, the hour when bread comes out of the oven, the sting of a fever, the sudden courage that arrives when there is no time to be afraid.

Who and what it gathers: smiths, cooks, soldiers, kiln-keepers, the restless and the brave. The Durn, who live by fire in the dark, and the Marcher hearth with its iron pot. Places: forges, kilns, deserts, the Emberfells, any room with a fire kept in it.

When it frays: ashlight. Fire that is cold, gives no warmth, consumes nothing, and spreads. Cinders that fall upward. Hearths that go out and cannot be lit by any means. People who are burnt out in the plain sense, with nothing left in them to kindle, and others who burn too fast and cannot be slowed.

Saying: Ember asks to be fed.

### Tide

Cold and water, patience, return, memory. Tide is the Thread of what goes out and comes back: the sea, the rain, the snow, grief, sleep, the slow cleaning of a wound. It is seen as blue green with a long slow pull.

How it is felt: the draw of the sea at the ankles, cold at the back of the throat, rain on a roof, the hush of snow, the way an old sorrow turns up in the same place every year.

Who and what it gathers: sailors, midwives, mourners, washerwomen, healers who work with cold and quiet. The Wakefolk above all, for whom Tide is the mother current. Places: coasts, meres, wells, snowfields, the Saltwrack and the wet heart of the Sallows.

When it frays: water that forgets to move, lying so still that a stone dropped in it makes no ring. Tides that climb and do not come back. Frost in high summer. Drowning on dry land, the lungs filling a mile from any water. Grief that does not ebb, in people and in places.

Saying: Tide takes and Tide returns.

### Storm

Lightning and wind, suddenness, speech, motion, music. Storm is the Thread of change by movement: the messenger, the shout, the crack that splits the sky and the pressure that comes before it. It is seen as white violet with a jagged, flickering grain.

How it is felt: hair rising on the arms, the drop in pressure before rain, the wind's voice in a chimney, the pleasure of running downhill, the moment a word is out of the mouth and cannot be called back.

Who and what it gathers: couriers, heralds, musicians, the quick and the talkative, Wakefolk sailors who read the wind, hunters who run. Places: tors and high places, headlands, the ridgelines of the Emberfells, the Saltwrack in a gale.

When it frays: dead air, where nothing moves and sound dies a yard from the mouth. Lightning that strikes and stays, a bolt standing in the sky for a season. Winds that blow inward to a single point and do not stop. People who cannot stop moving, or talking, or who are struck silent in mid-word and stay so.

Saying: Storm does not knock.

### Stone

The physical and the earth, weight, endurance, shelter, the body, law. Stone is the Thread of what stays: the mountain, the wall, the bone, the promise kept. It is seen as ochre and grey with a slow, heavy, almost unmoving grain.

How it is felt: cold flagstones underfoot, the silence of a mountain, the relief of a wall at the back, the ache of a long walk, the word given and held.

Who and what it gathers: builders, farmers, judges, wardens, the patient and the stubborn. The Durn are Stone's own people. Places: the Girdle, foundations, barrows, quarries, the roots of the Emberfells, any place where something was set down to last.

When it frays: the sag. Ground that goes soft and takes a cart to the axle. Walls that forget how to stand. Mountains that slump like wet bread. Bodies that forget their shape, joints that bend the wrong way, bones that remember being sand. People who lose their sense of place, and cannot say where they are even in their own kitchen.

Saying: Stone remembers what it was shown.

### Bloom

Life and growth, the will to become, healing, birth, the green. Bloom is the Thread of more: the seed, the sap, the mended cut, the child. It is seen as living green with a grain that branches.

How it is felt: the smell of cut grass, the itch of a wound closing, spring coming up through the soles of the feet, hunger in the good sense, the fierce tenderness of a parent.

Who and what it gathers: farmers, gardeners, healers, herders, midwives, the fertile and the generous. The Marchers of the Hallows, whose whole life is coaxing. The Order of the Mended Thread, who work in Bloom more than in any other Thread. Places: the Hallows, orchards, the Sallows, hospices, anywhere that is tended.

When it frays: blightbloom. Growth without pattern. Wheat that grows teeth. A forest that grows into itself until it is one tree with a thousand trunks and no light inside. A village swallowed in a night by a single rose. Tumours of green. And its mirror, which is as common: barrenness, soil in which nothing will take, wombs and fields alike.

Saying: Bloom does not ask whether.

### Umbra

Shadow and decay, rest, endings, secrets, the mercy of forgetting. Umbra is the Thread of what gives way so that other things may come: the leaf-mould that feeds the root, the sleep that ends the day, the grave. It is not evil and it is not Void. It is seen as a deep purple black with a soft, settling grain.

How it is felt: dusk, the cool under the eaves, the smell of a cellar, the relief of lying down, the kindness of not being looked at, the ache of a thing well finished.

Who and what it gathers: gravediggers, moth-keepers, keepers of secrets, nurses of the dying, the grieving and the discreet. Shades. The old families of Ilvary who keep the hooded Umbra lanterns. Places: the Sallows, barrows, cellars, the under-storeys of forests, the Hush at evening.

When it frays: shadows that detach and walk. Decay that stops, so that the dead do not rest and food never spoils and never nourishes. An evening that will not end. Hollowmen, which are people whose insides have given way while the outside kept on. Secrets that cannot be kept and secrets that cannot be told.

Saying: Umbra keeps what Lumen cannot bear.

### Lumen

Light and order, clarity, structure, revelation, law. Lumen is the Thread of the clean line: the dawn, the rule, the ledger, the moment of understanding. It is seen as pale gold or white with a straight, steady grain.

How it is felt: morning on a cold floor, the relief of knowing, the sting of being seen clearly, the pleasure of a thing in its right place, the weight of a just verdict.

Who and what it gathers: scholars, judges, clerks, architects, the exacting and the honest. The Lantern Chancery is Lumen's institution and Ilvary is Lumen's country. Places: the Lanterns, Cassevault, high clear places, courts and libraries.

When it frays: light with no source, a glare that comes from nowhere and casts no shadow. Too much order: things crystallise, every leaf on a tree identical, every face in a village the same face. Glasslings, which are creatures gone over to pure structure. People who can no longer lie, or change their minds, or forgive. And its mirror: lights that go out, and in their going nothing holds together.

Saying: Lumen shows; it does not choose.

### Void

Chaos and unmaking, and also the ground of all possibility. Void is the space between Threads, without which nothing could be woven at all. It is the room the Loom was given. It is feared more than any Thread and it is as necessary as any. Weavers who try to describe its colour say it is the colour of a closed eye.

How it is felt: vertigo, the pause between two breaths, the open sea at night, the feeling of being entirely unobserved, the blank before a thought.

Who and what it gathers: mystics, the Loosed, some Wakefolk who sail the Wake looking for its edge, Shades, the very old and the very tired. Places: the Fray, the deep sea, the Hush, the Needle's Eye, the quiet Ninth Well at Ninewells.

When it frays: holes. Places where the Loom shows through and there is nothing behind it. Things that forget they exist and go. The Unmade, which are what is left when a Pattern has been taken apart and did not stop moving. The Fray in the east is Void frayed on a scale no one has measured.

Saying: Void is the room the Loom was given.

### Mind

Thought, will, memory, names, the arcane. Mind is the Thread that knows it is a Thread; it is pattern that recognises pattern. All conscious weaving is done through Mind, whatever other Thread is drawn. It is seen as silver with a grain that turns back on itself.

How it is felt: the moment of recognition, the name on the tip of the tongue, a dream remembered at noon, the hard pleasure of a problem solved, the way a place can seem to remember you.

Who and what it gathers: scholars, Weavers of every kind, Binders and Kindlers in particular, the Spindlewatch, dreamers, liars, teachers. Places: libraries and vaults, the Spindle, the Echoes of the Hush, anywhere many people have thought the same thought for a long time.

When it frays: thoughts loose in the air, so that a whole village thinks with one head. Places that replay what happened in them, over and over, which Marchers call Echoes. Names that slip off their owners. Dreams that leak into the waking street. Madness, in its old sense of a mind that has come unwoven from the body that carries it.

Saying: Mind is the Thread that knows it is a Thread.

## Patterns and Skeins

A Pattern is any woven thing: a pebble, a storm, a song, a grudge, a city. Every Pattern has a proportion of Threads and a particular way they are tied. Two oaks are both mostly Bloom and Stone, but they are not the same Pattern any more than they are the same tree. A Weaver who reads a Pattern sees its Threads and its ties and, with practice, its history, because ties remember how they were made.

A living person's Pattern is called their HollowWake. Everyone has one. In most people the Skein is close-woven: tight, finished, holding its shape for a lifetime and changing only slowly with age and grief and love. In a Weaver the Skein has a loose Thread, sometimes several, which is why they can reach into the Loom at all. A Weaver's Skein grows as they weave. It takes on more Threads, more ties, more plies, and it becomes more complex and more particular, until an old Weaver's Skein is a thing no one else could have made. This growth is the long game of a Weaver's life.

## Weavers

A Weaver is a person who can consciously alter a Pattern. Most are born to it; a few are made, usually by touching a Fount at the wrong moment, or the right one. Since the Unraveling more Weavers have been born in every generation, and in the Weftmarches more than anywhere, which is one of the reasons people go there and one of the reasons other people worry.

Weaving has four movements, which every tradition teaches under different names. The Reach: sensing the Threads around you and in you. The Draw: taking hold of a Thread and pulling it toward a shape. The Cast: letting it go into the world as a Weave. The Fall: the moment the Weave ends and its Threads return to the Loom. A Weaver who does not let the Fall happen, who holds a Weave past its time, will find it fraying in their hands.

Weaving costs. A Weave draws on the Weaver's own Skein, and a Weaver who overdraws grows thin: pale, forgetful, cold, slow to heal. Rest restores it. So does a Fount. So does, in time, simply being a bigger Pattern than you were.

Non-Weavers call Weavers loose-threads, half in affection and half in warning. Weavers call non-Weavers close folk, or the close-woven, and mean no insult by it.

## Weaves

A Weave is a deliberate act of weaving, cast by a Weaver. These are the game's skills. A Weave draws chiefly on one Thread, though many draw on two, and it has a shape: a lash, a wall, a step, a mending, a veil. Weaves are taught, stolen, discovered and sometimes dreamed. Most Weavers learn their first from whoever taught them to Reach.

Weaves have common names, which are plain words in the Common tongue (Kindle, Undertow, Barrowgrip, Duskveil), and some have formal names in the tradition that first recorded them, which are longer and often honour a teacher. The naming conventions are in naming.md.

## Glyphs

A Glyph is a bound shape that changes how a Weave behaves. These are the game's skill modifiers. Where a Weave is the act, a Glyph is a knot tied in the act: a Fork splits it, a Ring spreads it, an Anchor holds it in place, an Echo makes it repeat, a Needle makes it pierce, a Burr makes it cling. Glyphs are named for shapes because that is what they are.

Weavers carry their Glyphs in their Skein, but most also keep a physical record. Marcher Weavers carry a cord with knots tied in it, one for each Glyph they hold, and will not let it be touched. Ilvarene Weavers keep theirs in a small bound book. Wakefolk tattoo them. The Durn cut them into the haft of a tool. Losing the record does not lose the Glyph, but Weavers are superstitious about it anyway.

## Reweaving

Reweaving is the craft of unpicking and re-binding the Threads in matter. This is the game's crafting. A Reweaver takes a thing apart at the level of its Threads and binds those Threads into something else. Done well it is the most useful craft in Aureth. Done badly it is how one gets a sword that is cold to the touch and will not take an edge, or a coat that remembers being a wolf.

Knots shed loose Thread as they turn, in a form Reweavers call floss: fine stuff, often visible as a drift of coloured light or a residue on stone and leaf, which carries one Thread strongly and is the raw material of the craft. Things recovered from Knots and Landmarks also carry Thread, sometimes richly. A Reweaver spins floss into twine, which is Thread made stable enough to bind, and binds twine into a Pattern that will hold it.

The tools are old and the names older. The distaff holds a Pattern open while it is unpicked. The shuttle carries the new Thread in. The heddle is the frame that holds the whole work apart so that the Reweaver can see what they are doing; by extension a Reweaver's workshop is called a Heddle, and the master of one a Heddle-master or Heddle-mistress. Rewoven things are said to be wrought in whatever Thread was bound into them: an emberwrought blade, a tidewrought cloak, a stonewrought door.

## Founts and Holdings

A Fount is a wellspring where the Loom surfaces and can be touched. Each has its own look, depending on the Threads that rise there: a pool with light moving under it, a cold well that does not reflect the sky, a vent of warm air that smells of a forge, a stone that hums when it is touched, a tree whose sap runs silver. At the heart of each is what the Wakefolk call its Eye, the place where the Threads can be seen directly. People who have looked into an Eye all say the same thing in different words: that it felt like a hand that had just let go.

Founts were known long before the Unraveling. The Concord chartered them and built Lanterns beside them. Many went dark in the tear and have been waking since, one by one, and the waking has quickened in the present generation.

A Weaver may bind a Fount. Binding is a rite, called the Vigil: the Weaver, or a company of Weavers together, stays with the Fount through its quiet hours and ties their Skein into its Pattern, and if the Fount takes them, it answers to them from then on. Whoever has bound a Fount is its Holder. The land a Fount's Pattern reaches is its Reach, and a bound Reach is a Holding. Within a Holding the Holder can shape the land, raise structures by weaving them up out of the ground's own Pattern rather than building them, set decrees called Writs that the land itself helps enforce, and draw the Fount's yield, which is called its Welling: floss, twine, water, light, warmth, whatever rises there.

Binding is not conquest. The Fount is not taken; it consents, or it does not. But a binding can be contested. A challenger may sit a counter-Vigil, called an Unbinding, at the Fount, and try to fray the Holder's tie and set their own; the Holder may resist with wards, with allies, and with their own presence. A binding also lapses if its Holder dies or is too long absent, and an unbound Fount runs wild: its Reach breeds Knots. So binding Founts is a civic act as much as an ambition, and a well-held Holding is a quiet neighbourhood.

Some Founts are bound in common. The Wellmother at Ninewells was bound by nine Weavers together, and the binding is renewed every year by the whole town at the Selvage, so that no single hand can hold it. This is rare, difficult, and the reason Ninewells is safe.

## Knots

A Knot is a place where Threads tangled during the Unraveling and never settled. These are the game's procedurally generated places: dungeons, anomalies, wild pockets. The Loom is always trying to resolve a Knot, pulling Threads through it, and so a Knot turns: each time it is entered it has re-tangled, and it is never quite the same twice. Marchers say you never walk the same Knot twice, and mean it literally.

Knots come in kinds, and the kinds have names:

- A Tangle is a wild pocket, often small, where the land has gone strange: a field that is always evening, a copse where it rains upward. Dangerous mostly by accident.
- A Snarl is a Knot that has drawn hostile Patterns into itself and holds them. These are the dungeons. A Snarl has a heart, called its Snag, where the tangle is tightest; cut the Snag and the Snarl slips, collapsing in on itself, shedding its floss, and beginning to re-tangle.
- A Thinning is a Knot where the Loom has worn thin rather than tangled: an anomaly, a place where the rules bend. Sound arrives late. The Warp can be seen in daylight. Weaves cast there do things they do not do elsewhere.
- A Burl is a Knot that has set: hardened, stable, rich in Thread, and rare. Burls are the prizes, and the quiet ones can be lived in.

Knots breed creatures. The Tangled are beasts and people and sometimes things whose Patterns were caught in a Knot and re-tied: cinderhounds, stoneboars, the drowned, thornwights, hollowmen, glasslings. The Unmade are something worse, and come out of the Fray. The Remnants are not Knot-born at all but older: wrought servants of the Concord, still keeping Patterns that no one asked them to keep.

Each region has its own kinds of Knot and its own names for them. Those are in regions.md.

## Landmarks

A Landmark is a fixed, deliberately made place that survived the Unraveling. These are the game's hand-placed locations. They do not turn. They were made on purpose by a hand that meant them to last, and they did.

There are three ages of Landmark. Firstworks were there before any people, and were made by the Loomwright or by nothing at all: the Spindle, the Cinderstair, the laid rivers. Concord works were made in the long age before the tear: the Lanterns, the Loomroads, the Vaults, Greyheron Light. Mending works were made after, by people trying to hold the world together: the Last Stitch above all.

Landmarks are found, explored, mapped, fought over and sometimes lived in. Many are still lost. Finding one is the best day of a Weaver's year.

## How the close-woven understand all this

Most people in Aureth are not Weavers and will never see a Thread. They are not ignorant of the Loom; they live in it. A farmer knows the grain of a field the way a Weaver knows its Pattern, and gets the same harvest. A fisherwoman knows which currents return. A mason knows which stone remembers.

Close folk speak of the Threads constantly and plainly. A hot-tempered child has too much Ember in her. A house that never gets warm is Tide-sick. A man who will not change his mind is all Stone, and a woman who will not stop talking is Storm's own. Everyone knows the nine and the order they are sung in, because the year is counted by them and children learn the count before they learn to read:

Lumen first to light the year,
Tide comes back and Storm comes near,
Bloom to grow and Ember burn,
Stone to stand and Umbra turn,
Mind to count what Void unmakes,
and five loose days the Selvage takes.

Close folk avoid Knots unless they are flossers, who harvest them for a living and die young. They hold Founts in awe and will not spit near one. They treat Weavers as neighbours with a dangerous trade, like a smith or a midwife, and they are right to.

## Reverence

There are no gods in Aureth who answer prayers, and the peoples know it. What there is instead is reverence: for the Loom, for the absent maker, for the Threads, and for the edges of things. It takes a different shape in each people.

Marchers tie knots. A knot-prayer is a cord with a knot tied in it for a wish, a thanks, a fear or a name, kept through the year and untied on the last day of the Selvage, when the year is unwoven and rewoven. Every Marcher doorframe has a cord on it. The Knotwives, who are folk-weavers and midwives and licensed by nobody, tie knots for people who cannot. Marchers say grace to the Quiet Hand and keep the nine wells at Ninewells swept.

Ilvary has the Mending Houses, a church without a god. Their creed is the Rule of the Needle: mend what you can; weave what you must; unpick nothing you cannot re-bind. Their houses are hospices and schools and courts, their symbol is a needle, and their highest act is to close a Thinning or heal a Tangled thing. The Lantern Chancery is a secular body but keeps the Mending Houses' calendar and burns their candles.

The Wakefolk give to the Wake. Every voyage begins with something thrown into the sea: a coin, a lock of hair, a name spoken once. They hold the western sea to be the wake of the maker's leaving, Tide its surface and Void its depth, and their wakesongs are both hymns and charts. They do not fear Void as other peoples do; they say it is only deep.

The Durn keep stone-silence. They do not name the maker aloud. They hold that the Loomwright is in the stone, moving at stone's pace, and that the right form of reverence is patience: to delve carefully, to leave the good seam for the grandchildren, to say less than you know. A Durn funeral is a stone set down and nothing said.

The Loosed have no rites and say they need none. They sit at the Last Stitch and look east.

And Weavers of every people have a faith of their own that none of them would call a faith. It is in the saying already given, and in the way they go quiet at a Fount, and in the fact that every one of them, sooner or later, sits in the dark and feels the Loom answer, and knows that no one is holding the other end.
