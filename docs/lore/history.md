# History: From the Setting to the Present Year

## How time is counted

Aureth keeps two counts, and the seam between them is the Unraveling.

The older is the Concord Count, written C, which runs from the swearing of the Concord of Nine Hearths at Cassevault. It is the count of the old world, and the Lantern Chancery still uses it for anything that happened before the tear.

The younger is the Mending Count, written M. The Loom tore on the ninth day of Voidspan in the year C 1211. The Order of the Mended Thread later fixed the start of its count at the Selvage that followed, so that M 1 is the first full year after the tear. Everyone uses the Mending Count now, including the Chancery, which resents it. The present year is M 309.

### The year

A year is nine Spans of forty days, each under a Thread, followed by the Selvage: five days that belong to no Span, when the year is said to be unwoven and rewoven. In some years the Spindlewatch declares a sixth Selvage day, when the Spindle's shadow falls behind the count. The Spans run in the order of the seasons, which is also the order in which children sing them:

1. Lumenspan, when the light returns. Late winter.
2. Tidespan, the thaw and the rains.
3. Stormspan, the spring winds.
4. Bloomspan, the growing.
5. Emberspan, high summer.
6. Stonespan, harvest and the setting of the year.
7. Umbraspan, leaf-fall.
8. Mindspan, the long nights of talk and tale.
9. Voidspan, deep winter, the year's unmaking.

Folk divide each Span into its rising, the first twenty days, and its falling, the last twenty. A date is written in the manner of "the twelfth of Bloomspan, M 309". The day of the Unraveling is remembered as Ninth Void, and on that day no one in Aureth weaves.

## The eras

### The Setting

Before any count, the Loomwright set the Loom. This is myth, and every people tells it differently, but the bones are the same. First the Warp was laid, the long fixed threads, which can still be seen in the northern sky. Then nine Threads were spun, no more and no fewer. Then the Weft was carried across the Warp, and the world came up in it: the Hem to the south, the Wake to the west, the Girdle to the north, and to the east, whatever was east before the Fray. The Firstworks were made then: the Spindle, the Cinderstair, the laid rivers, the folded hills, and others still unfound. Then the maker stepped away.

The Marchers say the Quiet Hand set the table and went out. The Mending Houses say the Absent finished the work and was not needed. The Wakefolk say the maker walked west across the water and the sea is the wake of that walking. The Durn say the Slow lay down in the stone and is lying there still. The Loosed say the shuttle was dropped, and on purpose. No one says the maker died, and no one says the maker will return, except in the Marches, in the small hours, when the hopeful say it quietly.

### The Whole Cloth

No people in Aureth remembers arriving. Each remembers waking. The Durn woke in the stone, in the dark under the Girdle, and dug toward warmth. The Wakefolk woke on the water, in boats they had not built. The Ilvarene woke on the Hem coast in a light with no sun in it, and when the sun rose they were already standing. These are the old peoples. The Marchers are not old; they are everyone who came later and stayed.

The long age before the Concord is called the Whole Cloth, because the Loom was whole. Patterns held. Nothing turned. Weavers were few and were called Wellsingers, because they sat at Founts and were told things, and most of what the old peoples know of the Loom they learned that way. Founts were sacred and unbound. There were no Knots. There was nothing in the world that was not as it had been made, except the peoples, who were making themselves.

The first binding was done by Aurelis Aurevant, a Wellsinger of the Hem coast, who sat a Vigil at the Fount later called Font Aurelle and did not come away until it answered her. Around it she raised the first wrought stones of Cassevault. That is the first act of the Ilvarene story and the first thing the Chancery teaches its clerks.

### The Concord Age

In the year that became C 1, nine Weaver-houses of the Hem coast, each devoted to a Thread, swore the Concord of Nine Hearths at Cassevault: Aurevant for Lumen, Belcastre for Mind, Castellane for Stone, Durenne for Ember, Mirevault for Tide, Solenne for Bloom, Valcoure for Storm, Orriaux for Umbra and Quillane, the quiet house, for Void. The Concord was a pact to bind Founts in common counsel rather than in rivalry, and to weave in the open rather than in secret. It held for twelve centuries.

The Concord raised the First Lantern at Cassevault within its first fifty years: a Lumen working so steady that Patterns for a day's ride around it did not drift. The Lanterns are the Concord's signature. They steadied the Loom, lit the roads, and marked the chartered Founts. By the middle of the Concord Age there was a Lantern beside every great Fount in Ilvary, Loomroads between them, and Vaults beneath the cities to hold what the Weavers had learned.

The Chancery of Lanterns was founded in C 112 to keep the charters: which Fount was bound, by whom, under what Writs. From keeping charters it came to issue them, and from issuing them it came to decide who was fit to receive one. By the late Concord the Chancery licensed Weavers as well as Founts, and a Weaver without a Lantern's seal was a Weaver without a Fount.

The Concord treated with the Durn at Scarrow Gate in C 388 and with the Wakefolk by the Tithe of Salt in C 410, and both treaties held, more or less, until the tear. In the seventh century of the count the Concord pushed north and east into what it called the Eastmarch, a wet green borderland between the Girdle and the sea, and lit Lanterns there: one beside a Fount among the alders that the Menders now hold, one on the sea-stack at Greyheron, and a waystation at Candlehall on the Loomroad. The Eastmarch was never rich and never quite tamed. Its Founts were many and strange. The Concord chartered the ones it could reach and left the rest.

The High Concord, from about C 900 to C 1100, is the age the Ilvarene mourn. The Vaults were full. The Remnants were wrought, servants of stone and Lumen that kept the roads and did not tire. Weaving was a learned art with schools and ranks and a literature. It was also, by then, a closed one. The nine houses married within the Concord, the Chancery's seals went to the houses' children, and the Loom, the Spindlewatch says, grew tight. Fewer Weavers were born in each generation of the late Concord than in the one before. The Concord took this for order.

In C 1180 Serapha Belcastre, Chancellor of the Ninth Hearth, proposed what the records call the Great Working. What it was for is the first thing the cultures disagree about. The Chancery's surviving minutes say it was to steady the whole Loom at once, as the Lanterns steadied their districts: a Lantern for the world. The Loosed say it was to call the maker back. The Menders say it was to make a Fount where there had been none, and the Wakefolk say it was to reach the Selvage. The Working was prepared for thirty years. It was performed at Cassevault, in the Hall of the Nine Hearths, with every house present, on the ninth day of Voidspan, C 1211.

### The Unraveling

The Loom tore.

Every people has its account, and they do not fit together, and that is the point. In the east the sky opened along a seam, the way cloth opens when it is cut on the bias, and the opening is the Fray. Every Lantern east of the Girdle's foot went out in the same instant. Every Fount in the Eastmarch went dark. The Remnants on the eastern roads stopped, and when they moved again they did not know what they were for. Weavers across Aureth felt their Skeins pulled, hard, toward the east; those who could not hold were pulled out of themselves and died standing, and are remembered as the Drawn. In the Girdle, the Durn say, the whole range rang like a struck bell, once, and seams that had been worked for a thousand years closed as if they had never been opened. On the Wake the sea went flat and did not move for nine days, and the Wakefolk call those days the Stillwater and do not sail on their anniversary.

In Cassevault the First Lantern guttered and held. The Hall of the Nine Hearths was found empty in the morning, every house's delegation gone, the fire still burning, the chairs pushed in. Nothing in it was broken. The city was untouched. The tear had opened four hundred miles away.

In the days that followed, Knots appeared everywhere. Fields tangled. Rivers turned. Towns woke to find the orchard was a Snarl and the well was an Eye. The Eastmarch emptied in a season, those who could leave leaving and those who could not becoming, many of them, the first of the Tangled. The Concord was finished, though it took the Chancery a generation to say so.

### The Loose Years

The first seventy years of the Mending Count are called the Loose Years, and are not much written about, because few people were writing. Cassevault closed its gates and kept the First Lantern burning and let the countryside look after itself. The Durn sealed Ondur and did not open it to outsiders for a century. The Wakefolk, who had lost half their boats to the Stillwater, put the rest to sea on the Long Sailing, west toward the Selvage, to find out whether the world still had an edge; most never came back, and those who did came back changed, with wakesongs that no one had sung before.

In Ilvary it was the time of the Needle-burnings. Weavers were blamed, which was not unreasonable, and burned, which was. The nine houses were hunted. Those who survived did so in the Mending Houses, which were then small hospices that took anyone in and asked nothing, and in the hills. Of the nine houses, Belcastre's name was struck from every Lantern in Ilvary by the Chancery's own order and is borne by no one now; Quillane, the quiet house, went quiet and was not heard from again; and Valcoure scattered into the hills, where its name survives only on villages. The other six, Aurevant, Castellane, Durenne, Mirevault, Solenne and Orriaux, kept their names through the Loose Years, in hospices and in hiding.

### The Mending

In M 71 Benoic Solenne, a Bloom-weaver of the hospice at Mirevault, walked east with a company of Weavers from every surviving tradition to the edge of the Fray, which had been widening a mile a year, and did the thing for which the whole age is named. They planted the Last Stitch: a line of needles taller than Lantern towers, wrought of Lumen-stone, bound through with Bloom and Stone, running north to south across the Fray's western edge. Nobody knows exactly what the Needle-rite did. What everyone knows is that the Fray stopped widening, and has not widened since.

The Order of the Mended Thread was founded three years later from the company that came back. Its creed, the Rule of the Needle, is the first new scripture of the Mending Count, and the only one.

The Chancery was restored at Cassevault in M 93 and issued the Charter of Lanterns, which held that no Fount in Ilvary might be bound except by a sealed Weaver, and that every binding was a loan from the Chancery. It was the old Concord rule with the Concord taken out, and it is still in force in Ilvary.

The Relighting followed slowly. Mirevault's Lantern was re-lit in M 140, the first since the tear, and the Chancery has been re-lighting since, one tower a generation. The Durn reopened Scarrow Gate in M 184 and resumed trade in Stone and Ember floss. The Wakefolk who came home from the Long Sailing settled the Scatter again and began to say, carefully, that the Selvage was real and that they had seen it from a distance.

### The Marching

Around M 200 people began going back into the Eastmarch, and the people who went were not the people who had left. They were Ilvarene without seals, Wakefolk without boats, Durn who had quarrelled with their seams, hospice orphans, flossers, Knotwives, and anyone else with a reason to be where the Chancery was not. They called the country the Weftmarches, because the weft shows there: the Patterns are loose, the Knots are many, and the Founts wake easily. The name is half a joke and half a warning. The people who stayed call themselves Marchers.

Hesper Wende mapped the river that bears her name in M 212, and the first steads went up in its valley that year. In M 226 nine Weavers, one for each Thread, sat a single Vigil together at a Fount where nine springs rose in a ring and bound it in common, each tying in a Thread, the ninth giving her name into the binding and never speaking it again. They are the Nine Who Stayed, and the town that grew around the Wellmother is Ninewells.

The Weftmarch Compact was sworn in the Wellhouse at Ninewells in M 241 by the steads of the Hallows, and later by most of the settlements of the Marches: that the Founts of the Marches are a commons, that a Vigil is the only title to one, that the Hedge will answer when any stead calls, and that no Chancery seal is needed to weave here. The Compact has never been accepted by the Chancery and has never been successfully challenged by it.

In M 268 Varu Longwake, a wake-house matriarch of the Scatter, obtained a Chancery charter for a trading company at Lowkeel on the Saltwrack coast, to harvest the Knots of the Marches and ship their floss south. The Longwake Company is the richest thing in the Weftmarches, and the Compact has disputed its charter since the day it was sealed.

The Order of the Mended Thread bound Alderheart in the Sallows in M 275 and raised the Hospice there, the first Fount in the Marches held by a southern power. In M 287 the Chancery sent its first Marchwarden, re-lit Candlehall, and announced that the Charter of Lanterns applied in the Weftmarches. The Licensing Dispute that followed is the shape of Marcher politics to this day.

In M 294 the first Loosed came back across the Last Stitch. They had gone into the Fray and not died, and they said the Fray was not a wound, and they said the Loom should be let go. One of them, who had been Severin Mirevault and was now Sev, spoke in the Wellhouse at Ninewells for an hour and was not thrown out, which the Chancery has not forgiven the Compact for.

### The present

Since about M 301 something has been changing, and the Spindlewatch has a word for it: the Loosening. Knots turn faster than they did. Founts that were dark since the tear are waking; Skyfold in the Emberfells woke in M 303 with a sound that was heard in Ninewells. More Weavers are born each year in the Marches than were born in a generation of the late Concord, and more of them are strong. The Warp, the Spindlewatch says, is drifting, by a hair, in the northern sky.

Nobody agrees what it means. The Chancery says it is the beginning of a second tear and that only chartered order will hold the cloth. The Compact says it is the Loom healing and that healing is loose. The Company says it is a harvest. The Menders say it is both wound and healing and that the work is the same either way. The Loosed say it is the Loom learning to weave itself, and that it will not need Weavers much longer, and that this is good.

Into this, in the present year, new Weavers arrive at Ninewells.

## The explanations of the Unraveling

The true cause of the Unraveling is not known, and the lore of Hollow Wake must never settle it. Four explanations are held in earnest by the peoples of Aureth, and each explains something the others cannot.

### The Hubris

Held by the Lantern Chancery and most of Ilvary. The Great Working tore the Loom. Nine houses reached for the whole cloth at once and the cloth could not take it. The Chancery struck Belcastre's name from the Lanterns for this, and its whole doctrine of seals and charters follows from it: weaving must be licensed because unlicensed weaving ended the world.

What it explains: the Working and the tear fell on the same day. What it cannot explain: why the tear opened four hundred miles from the hall where the Working was done, and why that hall was untouched. The Chancery's answer is that a Working on the whole Loom tears the cloth where it is weakest, not where the needle goes in.

### The Letting Go

Held by Marchers, by many in the Mending Houses, and in a sharper form by the Loosed. The maker, absent, had been holding the Loom all along, the way a hand holds a shuttle that has stopped; and on Ninth Void the hand let go. The Working was at most the occasion, not the cause. In the Marcher telling this is grief: the Quiet Hand has gone further off. In the Mending Houses it is a task: the Absent has left the mending to us. In the Loosed telling it is a gift: the Dropped Shuttle was dropped so that the Loom could learn to weave itself, and the Knots and the Founts and the new Weavers are the Loom learning.

What it explains: the Loosening, and why the Loom answers Weavers more readily every year. What it cannot explain: why the Fray opened in one place, and why the Last Stitch worked.

### The Thing Beneath

Held by the Wakefolk, and feared by everyone else in the small hours. There is something under the Loom. The Wake is deep, and Void is deeper, and in the Stillwater's nine days something surfaced or was let in. The Fray is not a tear but a mouth, or a wound where a mouth bit, and the Last Stitch did not mend it but only closed it, the way a hand holds a wound shut. The Wakefolk who came back from the Long Sailing will not say what they saw beneath the boats, only that it was patient.

What it explains: the Unmade, which do not behave like anything born of a tangle, and the way the Fray looks from the Last Stitch at night. What it cannot explain: why nothing has come through since. The Wakefolk answer is that nothing needs to hurry.

### The Wearing

Held by the Durn, who do not argue about it. Nothing caused the Unraveling. Cloth wears. The Loom is older than any count and had been fraying for an age; the Lanterns were a patch, and patches fail, and the Concord mistook its patch for the cloth. The Working did not tear the Loom; it was merely the last weight on a thread already thin. The Durn say the tear was always going to open in the east because the east was the oldest part of the weave, which is why the Girdle rang: the mountains were the hem that held.

What it explains: the slow calming of the Loose Years without anyone's help, and why the Knots have never spread as far as the first Menders feared. What it cannot explain: the Loosening, which the Durn observe without comment, and which worries them more than they say.
